The NVC process focuses attention on four key components:

  1. Observations: What we see or hear that affects our well-being, without judgment.
  2. Feelings: How we feel in response to these observations.
  3. Needs: The underlying universal human needs connected to our feelings.
  4. Requests: Specific, concrete actions we ask of others to enrich our lives

Moralistic Judgments:

This involves judging others as “wrong” or “bad” when their actions don’t match our values. This includes blame, insults, put-downs, labels, criticism, comparisons, and diagnoses. When we use this language, we focus on classifying and judging people (“who is what”) instead of understanding their needs

Instead of judging people, we can try to express what we need. When we do this, it creates understanding and connection. For example, instead of saying, “Violence is bad,” you might say, “I feel scared when violence is used to solve problems because I believe conflicts can be resolved peacefully.”

Simple Example

Imagine we want your partner to show more affection, but they’re not doing it. Instead of saying, “You’re aloof and insensitive,” you could express your need like, “I feel sad and lonely when I don’t get enough affection. I need more closeness.”

The main idea is that moralistic judgments focus on labeling people negatively, while expressing needs focuses on understanding each other better and creating a more compassionate connection.

Denial of Responsibility

This is when we pretend we have no control over what we think, feel, or do. It’s like saying:

  • “I have to do this” (like we don’t have a choice).
  • “You made me feel bad” (blaming others for how we feel).

Why This is a Problem: When we say things like this, we’re not taking responsibility for our own actions. It makes us feel like we’re not in control, which can lead to feeling stuck or helpless.

The Main Idea:

We have to realize that we choose how we act and feel. Even if it’s tough, we are responsible for our own choices.

Example

  • A teacher might say, “I have to give grades because the school says so.”
  • Need to say like this I choose to give grades because I believe it helps students understand their progress, and I want to keep my job so I can continue teaching and making a positive impact.

Observing Without Evaluating

It means simply stating what is actually happening in a situation, like what someone is saying or doing, without adding any opinions or labels to it. It’s about describing things that could be recorded with a video camera.

Example: Instead of saying “You are always busy,” which is a judgment, an observation would be: “Whenever I have observed Jack on the phone, he has spoken for at least thirty minutes” or “You are always busy” if used as an exaggeration. Or, instead of “Janice works too much,” an observation is “Janice spent more than sixty hours at the office this week”

What is Evaluation (or Judgment)?

This is when we mix our opinions, criticism, or analysis into what we are seeing or hearing. It’s like saying someone is “good,” “bad,” “lazy,” “stupid,” or “aggressive”.

Example: If you say “John was angry with me yesterday for no reason,” the phrase “for no reason” and assuming John was angry are evaluations. An observation would be, “John told me he was angry,” or “John pounded his fist on the table”. Similarly, calling someone “a good man” or saying “Doug procrastinates” are evaluations

When we combine observation with evaluation, people often hear criticism. This makes them defensive and less likely to listen to what we are truly trying to communicate.

Identifying and Expressing Feelings

The Cost of Unexpressed Feelings

In many cultures (like in schools), people are not taught how to talk about their feelings. Instead, we’re often taught to focus on what makes sense or what others think is right. This means we end up hiding our feelings, which causes problems in relationships.

** Feeling Disconnected at Work**

  • You work with someone who never asks how you’re doing. Instead of saying, “I feel ignored,” you might just think, “They don’t care about me.”

  • Over time, this builds walls between you and that person. If you don’t express that you feel neglected, the other person might not even realize their actions are making you feel this way.

  • Better Approach: “I feel unimportant and lonely when I don’t hear from you at work. I would really appreciate a bit more communication.”

When we express our real feelings, others can understand us better, and we can solve the problem more effectively.

Building a Vocabulary for Feelings:

Here’s a list of feelings, divided into two categories, to help make your feelings clearer.

When your needs are being met (positive feelings):

  • Happy: “I feel joyful when I spend time with my family.”
  • Excited: “I’m excited because I just got a new job!”
  • Grateful: “I feel grateful when my friend helps me out.”
  • Content: “I feel peaceful and content after spending the afternoon reading.”
  • Trusting: “I feel trusting when you keep your promises.”

When your needs are NOT being met (negative feelings):

  • Angry: “I feel angry when I’m interrupted while speaking.”
  • Frustrated: “I feel frustrated when I can’t get the work done on time.”
  • Lonely: “I feel lonely when I’m not able to hang out with friends.”
  • Sad: “I feel sad when we don’t talk as much as we used to.”
  • Anxious: “I feel anxious before big presentations because I’m worried I might mess up.”

By using these specific feelings words, you can express what’s really going on inside you. Instead of just saying, “I feel bad,” you can be more specific and say, “I feel frustrated and exhausted because I’ve been working overtime.”

Expressing how we feel is powerful because it helps us connect better with others. When we express our vulnerability (real feelings), others can see us for who we really are, and this builds understanding.

Example: Sharing a Feeling Can Improve Relationships

  • Let’s say a friend says something hurtful to you. Instead of thinking, “They’re mean,” or holding a grudge, you could say, “I feel hurt when you speak to me that way because I care about our friendship.”
  • This opens up the conversation, allows your friend to understand you, and possibly even fix things.

In short, expressing feelings helps you communicate more clearly and connects you with others in a deeper way.

Taking Responsibility for Our Feelings

When someone does something that upsets us, we often think it’s their fault we feel a certain way. But in reality, our feelings come from our needs whether those needs are met or not.

Blaming Ourselves

Imagine you missed an important deadline at work. You might think:

  • “I messed up, it’s my fault,” and you feel guilty and stressed.

But that feeling of guilt is coming from your need for self-respect or a sense of competence your need for being on top of things, which wasn’t met. It’s not just the missed deadline that caused your feelings, but your need to be responsible that wasn’t fulfilled.

Blaming Others

Now, let’s say your friend cancels plans with you at the last minute. You might think:

  • “You’re so selfish! You don’t care about me!” and feel angry or hurt.

But your feelings of anger are coming from your need for respect or connection that wasn’t met when your friend canceled. It’s not that your friend made you angry; it’s that your need for a reliable, caring friend wasn’t fulfilled in that moment.

Focusing on Your Own Needs and Feelings

Instead of blaming, you start thinking about what you need in this situation:

  • “I feel frustrated and disappointed because I need reliability and respect in my friendships.”

Now, you’re connecting your feelings to your own unmet need for respect and reliability, not blaming your friend.

Focusing on the Other Person’s Needs

Instead of just focusing on your own feelings, you could also try to understand why the other person did what they did:

  • “I wonder if you felt overwhelmed and needed space, and that’s why you canceled our plans?”

This shows compassion by trying to understand their needs, not just focusing on how their actions affected you.

NOTE: “What others do is not the cause of our feelings. Our feelings come from our own needs.”

Requesting That Which Would Enrich Life

Once we have clearly expressed our observations, feelings, and needs, we move to request an action that could help meet those nee

When you ask someone for something:

  1. Say what you want, not what you don’t want.
  2. Be clear and specific.
  3. Know what you’re really asking for.
  4. Ask them to reflect back if needed.
  5. Invite their honest response.
  6. Be clear with groups.
  7. Don’t guilt-trip respect “no”.
  8. Stay focused on connection, not control.

1. Use Positive Action Language

Concept:
Focus on what you want the other person to do, not what you want them to stop doing.

Why It Matters:
Negative statements (e.g., “Don’t be late”) leave the other person guessing. Positive requests give clear direction.

Ineffective Example:
“I don’t want you to be late.”

Effective Example:
“I’d like you to arrive on time for our meetings.”

Application:
Describe the action you wish to see. This reduces defensiveness and increases the likelihood of cooperation.

2. Make Requests Clear, Concrete, and Specific

Concept:
Vague or abstract requests lead to misunderstanding and frustration. Clearly describe the exact behavior you want.

Ineffective Example:
“I want you to be more responsible.”

Effective Example:
“I’d like you to wash the dishes after dinner each night.”

Insight:
Often, what sounds like a call for mutual respect or freedom may actually hide a desire for obedience or agreement. Bringing specificity to the surface helps clarify our true needs and promotes honest dialogue.

3. Be Conscious About What You’re Requesting

Concept:
Every time we express a feeling or need, we are implicitly requesting something in return—even if it’s simply empathy. Being conscious of this helps make our communication more effective.

Example:
Saying “I’m annoyed you forgot the butter” without stating a request might leave the listener unsure how to respond. What you really mean might be: “Would you be willing to go back to the store and get it?”

Application:
Before speaking, take a moment to ask yourself: “What exactly am I hoping the other person will do or say in response to this?”

4. Ask for a Reflection

Concept:
To ensure clarity, ask the listener to repeat back what they heard you say in their own words.

Why It Matters:
This helps avoid misunderstandings and allows you to correct any confusion early.

Example:
A teacher told a student he couldn’t participate in a sports event because of missing homework. The student misunderstood, believing he was being punished for bad work. The teacher asked him to repeat back what he heard, discovered the misunderstanding, and clarified her intent.

Application:
After a serious or important request, say: “Can you tell me what you heard me say?” This checks for mutual understanding.

5. Requesting Honesty

Concept:
After making a request, you may want to know how the other person feels about it. You can invite honesty by asking for specific types of responses.

Examples:

  • “How do you feel about what I just said?”
  • “Do you see any challenges with what I’m asking?”
  • “Would you be willing to do this by tomorrow?”

Application:
Encourage openness. This builds trust and gives the other person space to express their own needs and limits.

6. Making Requests of a Group

Concept:
When speaking to a group, it’s crucial to be clear about what kind of response you’re hoping for feedback, a decision, action, or simply listening.

Ineffective Example:
“We need to talk about racism in this organization.”

Effective Example:
“I’d like to discuss starting a diversity committee. Would anyone be willing to join me in organizing it?”

Application:
State clearly what action or input you want from the group. This avoids confusion and keeps the conversation productive.

7. Requests Versus Demands

Concept:
A true request allows for a “no” without punishment or guilt. A demand is when the speaker becomes critical, angry, or manipulative if their request is denied.

Key Difference:
The difference lies in how the speaker reacts when the request is not met.

Example (Request):
“I’m feeling lonely. Would you be willing to spend the evening with me?”
Response: “I’m really tired.”
Follow-up: “Thanks for telling me. Let’s find another time.”

Example (Demand):
“I’m lonely. Spend the evening with me.”
Response: “I’m tired.”
Follow-up: “You never care about me.”

Application:
Monitor your own response to a “no.” If you feel resentment, guilt, or judgment rising, it may not have been a genuine request.

8. Define Your Objective

Concept:
The goal of NVC is not to get others to do what we want through pressure or manipulation. The aim is to build connection, honesty, and mutual understanding

Guiding Question:
Am I making this request to meet only my needs, or to foster mutual well-being?

Warning Sign:
If you believe the request failed just because the other person said no, it may have been a demand in disguise.

Application:
Before making a request, ask yourself: “Am I okay if the answer is no? Am I open to understanding their needs, too?”

Receiving Empathically

Empathy is defined here as a respectful understanding of what others are experiencing. It is not about fixing, analyzing, or reassuring. Rather, it is about being fully present listening with one’s whole self and offering attention without interruption or judgment.

Common responses that are often mistaken for empathy include:

  • Advising: “You should…”
  • Consoling: “It wasn’t your fault.”
  • One-upping: “That reminds me of when I…”
  • Educating: “Here’s why that happened…”
  • Storytelling: “That reminds me of…”
  • Interrogating: “Why did you do that?”
  • Sympathizing: “Oh you poor thing.”
  • Shutting down: “Just forget about it.”
  • Correcting or fixing: “That’s not true, you’re fine.”

These responses distract from true empathy by shifting focus away from the speaker’s experience.

How to Receive with Empathy

1. Listen for Feelings and Needs, Not Judgments

When others speak, especially in emotionally charged language, the empathetic listener does not react to the words literally, but instead listens for what the person may be feeling and needing.

Example:

If someone says, “You never listen to me,” a literal interpretation may cause defensiveness. An empathetic interpretation might sound like:

“Are you feeling hurt because you’re needing more attention or understanding from me?”

The goal is not to agree or disagree, but to understand what the other person is experiencing.

2. Paraphrasing to Check Understanding

Paraphrasing is the practice of reflecting back what you’ve heard as a tentative question. This shows attentiveness and gives the speaker a chance to clarify.

Examples of Paraphrasing:

  • “Are you reacting to how many evenings I was away last week?”
  • “Are you feeling disappointed because you wanted more appreciation?”
  • “Are you asking me to explain why I said that?”

These reflections include guesses about the other person’s observation, feeling, need, or request.

When to Paraphrase:

  • When emotions are high
  • When you are unsure about what the speaker means
  • When the speaker explicitly asks you to understand
  • When communication feels unclear or stuck

Tone Matters:
Paraphrasing must be delivered gently and with humility. It is not a statement of fact, but a sincere attempt to understand.

3. Sustaining Empathy

Empathy is not a one-time act. It is an ongoing effort to stay with the other person’s experience. Often, we are tempted to shift the conversation back to ourselves or offer solutions prematurely.

To offer sustained empathy, we must allow the other person to fully express themselves before offering input. True empathy is often received when the other person:

  • Feels emotionally lighter
  • Stops speaking or slows down naturally
  • Expresses relief or gratitude

These signs indicate that the person has been heard and understood at a deeper level.

When You Can’t Offer Empathy

There are times when we are in too much emotional pain ourselves to offer empathy to others. In these moments, Rosenberg suggests three possible options:

  1. Emergency Self-Empathy Take a moment to identify your own feelings and needs. Offer yourself the same presence and care you would offer another. This can create enough inner clarity to continue the interaction empathically.

  2. Express Pain Without Blame (“Screaming Nonviolently”) If emotions are too intense, express your needs clearly but without blaming the other. For example:

    “I’m feeling overwhelmed and I really need some quiet. I’m not ready to keep discussing this right now.”

    This allows you to be honest while still respecting the other person.

  3. Take a Break If needed, remove yourself from the situation. Seek your own support before returning to the interaction. Attempting to offer empathy when you are emotionally depleted can be harmful to both parties.

Expressing Anger Fully

Anger is often seen as something to avoid or suppress, but in reality, anger is a signal from within that tells us important things about ourselves. Marshall Rosenberg explains that anger is not caused directly by others’ actions but by our own thoughts and judgments about those actions. It is a surface expression hiding deeper feelings and unmet needs.

Understanding Anger

  • Stimulus vs. Cause: Other people’s behavior is a stimulus, not the cause of our anger. What causes anger are the judgments and blame we place on others in our minds.
  • Example: A prisoner felt angry at prison staff, but the real cause of his anger was his own belief that they had no respect for human beings.
  • Example: The author was hit twice by children. The first time he judged the child harshly and felt rage; the second time, he felt no anger, even though the pain was worse. This showed that anger comes from how we interpret others’ behavior, not the behavior itself.

The Deeper Meaning of Anger

Anger signals unmet needs such as respect, safety, or fairness. Instead of using anger to blame or punish, we can use it as a clue to connect with those deeper needs.

How to Express Anger Fully (Four Steps)

  1. Pause and Breathe: When you feel anger rising, stop and take a moment to calm yourself instead of reacting immediately.
  2. Identify Judgmental Thoughts: Notice the specific judgments or blame you are thinking.
  3. Connect with Your Needs: Ask yourself what needs are not being met that underlie your anger.
  4. Express Feelings and Needs: Share your true feelings and the needs behind them clearly and without blame.

For example, instead of saying, “That was racist!” you might say, “When you ignored me and made that comment, I felt scared and hurt because I need to be treated with respect.”

Empathy Before Expression

Before sharing your feelings, try to empathize with the other person’s feelings and needs. This helps create understanding and makes it more likely they will listen to you.

  • Example: The author calmed his anger at a man making prejudiced remarks by first sensing the man’s frustration and pain, then sharing his own feelings once the man felt heard.

Take Your Time

Expressing anger mindfully takes practice. Using reminders or tools can help you slow down and respond with awareness rather than react impulsively.

Example

If you’re feeling angry because your friends are trolling you, here’s how you can handle it using the approach from Chapter 10 of Nonviolent Communication:

  1. Pause and breathe: Instead of reacting immediately or snapping back, take a moment to calm yourself. This helps you avoid saying something you might regret.
  2. Notice your judgmental thoughts: Ask yourself what judgments or labels you’re placing on your friends. Are you thinking, “They’re mean,” or “They don’t respect me”?
  3. Connect with your unmet needs: What needs are not being met when they troll you? Maybe you need respect, kindness, or feeling valued as a friend.
  4. Express your feelings and needs calmly: Share how their behavior affects you without blaming. For example, you could say,
    “When you make jokes at my expense, I feel hurt and disrespected because I really value kindness and respect among friends. I’d like us to have fun without putting each other down.”
  5. Offer empathy if they react defensively: If they respond with irritation, try to listen to their feelings and needs too. Maybe they don’t realize how their words affect you, or maybe they’re joking in a way they think is playful.

Connecting Compassionately With Ourselves

It teaches us how to be kind and gentle to ourselves to treat ourselves with the same care and understanding we want to give others. Being compassionate with ourselves is very important because if we are harsh inside, it’s hard to really be kind to other people.

Stop Being Mean to Yourself

  • Many people think they are “not good enough” or “stupid” when they make mistakes. This is like being mean to yourself.

  • Rosenberg says that when you say something mean about yourself, like “I’m dumb,” it actually shows that you have an unmet need — maybe you want to feel capable or respected.

  • Instead of calling yourself names, try to ask:
    “What do I really need right now?”
    This helps you understand yourself instead of feeling ashamed.

Example:
You forgot to send an important email and think, “I’m so dumb!”
Instead, ask, “What am I needing here?” Maybe you want to feel organized or reliable. Realizing this helps you feel better and find a solution, rather than beating yourself up.

Conflict Resolution and Mediation

The key is to focus on connecting as humans, understanding everyone’s feelings and needs, rather than just trying to win or compromise.

Human Connection is Most Important

  • Before trying to fix the problem, connect with each other as people.
  • When both sides feel heard and cared for, solutions come more easily.
  • The goal is not to make one person win or force anyone, but to find a way that respects everyone’s needs.

Example:
If two friends are fighting over who should pick the restaurant, instead of arguing about who’s right, they first share why the restaurant matters to them maybe one wants comfort food because they had a hard day, the other wants healthy food because of health concerns. Understanding each other makes it easier to find a good choice.

Five Steps of NVC Conflict Resolution

  1. Express your own feelings and needs clearly.
    Say what you feel and need, not what others did wrong.
  2. Listen for the other person’s real needs, even if they sound like complaints or judgments.
  3. Check that both sides understand each other’s needs correctly.
  4. Give empathy to both sides so everyone feels heard and understood.
  5. Suggest clear, positive actions that can meet both people’s needs.

Avoid blaming or saying someone is wrong during this process.

The Protective Use of Force

It talks about when using force might be necessary but how to do it in a way that fits with Nonviolent Communication (NVC). It explains the difference between using force to protect (which can be okay) and using force to punish (which causes harm and usually makes things worse).

When Might Force Be Needed?

  • Sometimes talking and understanding each other isn’t enough.
  • Maybe the other person doesn’t want to communicate.
  • Or there’s an immediate danger where waiting or talking could cause harm.

In these rare cases, some kind of force might be necessary to keep people safe or protect rights.
